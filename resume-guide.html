How resumes actually get screened

Most applications are first read by a recruiter, often after an applicant tracking system (ATS) has sorted or filtered them. The first read is short — usually well under a minute — and it answers three questions: Is this person in the right field? Are they at the right level? Is there evidence they can do this job?

That's why a resume isn't your life story. It's a short, skimmable argument for one kind of role. Everything on it should help answer those three questions quickly.

  • Put the job title you want in your headline so the right field is obvious.
  • Make your level visible: years, scope, team size, budgets.
  • Prove skills with results in bullets, not with a long skills list.

The structure that works

A strong resume has a predictable shape, so the reader finds what they need without hunting:

  1. Header — name, target title, phone, email, city, LinkedIn (and GitHub or portfolio if relevant).
  2. Summary — 2–3 lines: who you are for this role, your strongest skills, one real result.
  3. Experience (or Projects first if you're a fresher or switcher) — most recent first.
  4. Skills — grouped, only what you can discuss in an interview.
  5. Education and Certifications.

Freshers: one page. Most people with under 8 years: one page, two at most. Leave out photo, date of birth, marital status, religion, father's name, full address and the "declaration" — they cost space and add nothing.

Writing bullets that prove impact

Each bullet should show what you did, how, and what changed because of it. A simple pattern:

Action verb + what you did + for whom / with what + result (with a number)

Weak: Responsible for preparing weekly sales reports.
Strong: Automated the weekly sales report in Power BI for 14 regional managers, cutting preparation time from 7 hours to 1.

  • Start with a strong past-tense verb: Built, Led, Automated, Reduced, Launched, Negotiated.
  • Use numbers you can defend: users, rows, hours, %, ₹, team size, number of reports.
  • No exact number? Use a careful estimate ("~30%") or describe scale ("for all 4 regions") — never invent.
  • One idea per bullet, 10–30 words, 3–5 bullets for your latest role.

Standing out (honestly)

  • Tailor every important application. Mirror the job's wording where your experience truly matches; reorder bullets so the most relevant come first.
  • Show ownership. "Led", "owned", "designed" beat "assisted" — when it's true.
  • Add proof links. GitHub, a live dashboard, a portfolio case study or a published article make claims believable.
  • Quantify scope. Small numbers are fine. "Trained 6 new joiners" is better than "trained new joiners".
  • Cut generic claims. "Hard-working team player" is invisible. Results show those qualities for you.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Listing duties ("responsible for…") instead of results.
  • A skills list with 40 tools and no bullet that proves any of them.
  • Typos, inconsistent dates, mixed tense, or random fonts.
  • Two-column designs, tables, icons and text boxes that confuse an ATS.
  • Personal details (DOB, photo, religion) and a "declaration" at the end.
  • Keyword stuffing: copying the job description into white text or a hidden block. Recruiters notice, and it destroys trust.
  • An objective that talks about what you want instead of what you offer.

Different levels, different resumes

Students & freshers: Lead with skills and 2–3 strong projects (problem, tools, your part, result, link). Internships, freelance and college roles count as experience. Education can sit higher.

Career switchers: Write a summary that names the new role and connects your past to it. Put new skills and projects first, and rewrite old roles to highlight transferable work (analysis, stakeholders, process improvement).

1–8 years: Experience goes near the top. Show growth: promotions, bigger scope, harder problems.

Managers and senior people: Focus on the last 8–10 years, team and business results, decisions and influence. Fewer tools, more outcomes.

A 15-minute polish routine

  1. The 10-second test: give your resume to a friend for ten seconds. Can they say what job you want and your best result?
  2. The "so what?" test: read each bullet and ask "so what?". If there's no answer, add the result or cut it.
  3. The verb scan: check the first word of every bullet. Replace weak openers.
  4. The number scan: aim for numbers in at least half your bullets.
  5. Read it aloud to catch typos and clumsy sentences.
  6. Export to PDF and open it, to check spacing and page breaks.

Beyond the resume

  • LinkedIn: same story as your resume, with a headline that names your target role, an About section in first person, and a custom URL.
  • Referrals: a short, specific message to someone at the company beats a hundred cold applications.
  • Cover letters: keep them short, connect two real proof points to what the job needs.
  • Interview prep: prepare a STAR story (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every bullet on your resume — interviewers will ask.
  • Follow up politely about a week after applying.
